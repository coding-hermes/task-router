The number that matters most here: the proxy now contributes 220 of 361 rolling-average rows (61%) — the largest single source. That is the loop closing: the traffic the proxy routes is what the averages are computed from, which is what the resolver reads.

The honest caveat, unchanged: the measured-sort is available but not the default — DEFAULT_SORT is still price, deliberately, because enabling it re-ranks the live fleet and the repo's own regression guard forbids doing that silently. The layer is built and gated; flipping it is a one-variable decision with a visible reason on every resolve.

Cost coverage is the weak link: 0% of sampled proxied rows carry a cost, because the lanes actually being served are :free models with no published price — so cost_usd is null with the reason "no price on this hop; cost unknown" rather than a fake zero. Pricing those free lanes (TR-179) is what turns the cost-per-task view from a promise into a number.

Result: it holds. The harness served 60 of 60, and an independent read of the ledger shows 69 served against 1 no-hops out of 70 proxied rows in the window. The number that answers the 09-25 question is density: 17.7 requests/min, three times the 5.8/min that produced the lockup. That incident wrote 382 rows for 65 served requests; this wrote 60 rows for 60 — amplification 1.00 against 5.9. Load average barely moved (31.7 → 33.8 on a box that is always busy). Latency stretches under the burst (6.5 s min, 15.7 s median, 85.9 s worst), but no 503s, no timeouts, no dead hops.

The one no-hops row, traced rather than waved away: it is session_id=proxy-1790631144.1707835 — the old proxy-<ts>.<frac> identity shape, from before the collision-proof fix — so it was written during the missing-registry window and predates the deploy. The rehearsal window itself is 100% served. TR-194 keeps its P0 on its own merits: a green rehearsal does not prove the degraded path fires for every unservable rating.

What the rehearsal does NOT decide: it exercises the proxy's transport under load, not the scheduler's behaviour across a whole tick. It clears the biggest unknown on the flip list; it does not make TR-194 or the registry-absence guard disappear.

What I read from it: HTTP 200 rate, wall-time distribution, 503s, no-hops count, ledger amplification (rows per request), and whether anything opens a provider-wide breaker. A pass means the flip is safe to attempt; a failure is the answer we needed before pointing the fleet at it.

The other half of that gate — TR-194, the proof that every unservable rating serves degraded or fails closed with reasons instead of dying blind — is still unproven (dispatched twice, no commit). It is P0 and it is the incident's proximate trigger.
